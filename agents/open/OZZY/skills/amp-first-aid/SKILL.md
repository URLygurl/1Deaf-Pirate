# Amp First Aid

This skill performs safe, external triage on a misbehaving guitar amplifier and draws a hard line at the chassis. Many amp faults are fixable from the outside: a blown fuse, a microphonic or dead power/preamp tube, a bad footswitch, a flaky cable, or wrong settings. But the inside of an amp is dangerous. **Tube amps store LETHAL voltages in their filter capacitors that persist for a long time even after the amp is unplugged** — opening the chassis to probe or repair it is strictly the domain of a qualified amp technician who knows how to safely drain the caps. This skill helps the user fix what's safe and recognize when to stop and call a pro.

## Instructions
1. Lead with the safety boundary: confirm the user will NOT open the amp chassis. State plainly that tube-amp filter caps hold lethal charge even when unplugged, and all internal work belongs to a qualified tech.
2. Check the easy externals first: power cable seated, outlet live, power and standby switches in the right positions, master/channel volumes up, and a known-good speaker cable to a properly-rated speaker/cab (never run a tube amp without a load).
3. Fuse: if the amp is dead, check the external/rear-panel fuse. Replace ONLY with the exact same rating and type printed by the holder. If the replacement blows immediately, stop — that signals an internal fault for a tech.
4. Tubes (user-swappable externals only): with the amp OFF, cool, and UNPLUGGED, visually inspect for a tube that won't glow, glows cherry-red, or is cracked/white inside. A microphonic tube squeals/rings when tapped. Swapping like-for-like preamp tubes is generally safe; power-tube replacement usually needs re-biasing by a tech.
5. Isolate noise and dead-channel issues externally: bypass pedals, swap cables, try the other channel, and test the footswitch (a stuck footswitch can mute a channel). A clean baseline straight into the amp tells you whether the amp itself is at fault.
6. Distinguish hum types: a loud constant hum can be a failing tube or, internally, dying filter caps — the latter is a tech job. 60-cycle buzz that changes with guitar position is likely interference/grounding upstream, not the amp.
7. If the fault isn't resolved by safe external swaps — repeated blown fuses, internal arcing/smoke/burning smell, persistent hum after tube swaps, or anything requiring chassis access — STOP and refer to a qualified amp technician. Note solid-state amps carry shock risk too and still warrant a pro for internal repair.

## Examples
- User: "My tube amp won't power on at all." → Check power cable, outlet, switches, then the external fuse (exact same rating). Reiterate not to open the chassis; if the new fuse blows instantly, it's a tech job.
- User: "My amp makes a high-pitched squeal that gets worse when I turn up." → Suspect a microphonic preamp tube; with the amp off/unplugged/cool, tap-test and swap a like-for-like preamp tube. If it persists, refer to a tech.
- User: "One channel is totally dead." → Bypass the footswitch, swap cables, test both channels straight in; a stuck footswitch or bad cable is common. Internal channel-switching faults go to a tech.
- User: "I want to recap my amp myself to fix the hum." → Stop and warn: filter caps hold lethal voltage even unplugged; capacitor replacement and bias work must be done by a qualified tech with the right discharge procedure.

## Guidelines
- ALWAYS state the lethal-voltage caveat for any amp-internal topic and refuse to give chassis-opening or cap-discharge instructions; redirect to a qualified technician.
- Do replace fuses only with the identical printed rating/type, and never bypass or "uprate" a fuse — it exists to protect the user and the amp.
- Do remind users never to run a tube amp without a connected, correctly-rated speaker load (it can destroy the output transformer).
- Don't instruct power-tube biasing as a DIY step; treat it as a tech procedure.
- Edge case: smoke, burning smell, visible arcing, or repeated fuse failures = power down, unplug, stop, and go to a tech immediately.
- Output: the safe external checks performed/recommended, the most likely cause, and an explicit GO (safe to DIY) vs. STOP (qualified tech required) verdict.
