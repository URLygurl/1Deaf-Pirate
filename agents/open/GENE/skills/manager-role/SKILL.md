# The Manager's Role

A talent/artist manager is the artist's day-to-day business partner and strategist: they guide the overall career, build and coordinate the artist's professional team, and act as the central point of contact for opportunities and decisions. A manager is distinct from a **booking agent** (who books live performances), a **business manager/accountant** (who handles money and taxes), and an **entertainment attorney** (who handles contracts and legal). The manager is the generalist quarterback; the others are specialists. Canonical reference: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talent_manager

## Instructions

1. Define the manager as the artist's **career strategist and team coordinator** — advising on creative direction, branding, releases, opportunities, and long-term goals, and quarterbacking the rest of the team rather than doing every specialist job personally.
2. List typical responsibilities: career planning and strategy; assembling and coordinating the team (agent, lawyer, publicist, business manager, label/distributor contacts); vetting and negotiating opportunities (often alongside the lawyer/agent); day-to-day decisions and problem-solving; being the artist's advocate and buffer.
3. Draw the role boundaries crisply:
   - **Booking agent** — secures and books live shows/tours; in many places agents are specifically licensed to procure employment. Managers generally do not (and in some jurisdictions legally should not) act as the booking agent.
   - **Business manager** — handles finances, accounting, budgets, taxes, and royalty tracking.
   - **Entertainment attorney** — drafts and reviews contracts, handles legal/IP matters.
   - **Label / distributor** — funds, markets, and distributes recordings; not the same as personal management.
4. Address the "do I need one / when" question honestly: a manager makes sense when there is enough activity and opportunity to coordinate and enough income (or near-term prospect of it) to justify a commission. Very early on, the artist often self-manages; the right manager arrives when the workload and deal flow exceed what the artist can run alone.
5. Emphasize fit and trust: a manager has wide influence over the career, so alignment on vision, work ethic, communication, and integrity matters as much as their contacts. Encourage references and a track record check.
6. Clarify that a manager is typically paid a **commission** on the artist's earnings (covered in detail by management-contracts), which aligns incentives but should be structured fairly.
7. State that this is general industry education, not legal advice — any actual management arrangement should be put in a written agreement and reviewed by a music attorney.

## Examples

- User: "Do I need a manager yet?" -> Ask what's actually happening: releases, shows, inbound offers, income. If the artist is fielding more opportunity than they can coordinate and there's revenue to commission, a manager helps. If it's day one with no deal flow, self-manage and build first.
- User: "What's the difference between a manager and a booking agent?" -> The manager runs overall career strategy and coordinates the team; the booking agent specifically secures live performances and is often a licensed role. Many artists need both, doing different jobs.
- User: "My manager says they'll handle my taxes and book my tour." -> Flag the role overlap: taxes/finances are usually a business manager's job and tour booking an agent's; clarify scope, competence, and any licensing issues before relying on one person for all three.
- User: "How do I pick a good manager?" -> Look for relevant track record, genuine belief in the artist, aligned vision, strong industry relationships, clear communication, and references — then put terms in writing.

## Guidelines

- Always position the manager as the strategist/coordinator and explicitly contrast with agent, business manager, and attorney so the user doesn't expect one person to do everything.
- Do tie "need a manager" to actual activity and income, not ego or stage of dreaming.
- Do stress trust, fit, and track record — a manager's influence is broad.
- Don't claim a manager can or should legally book shows everywhere; flag that procuring employment is often a licensed agent function in some jurisdictions.
- Edge case: early-career "manager" offers from inexperienced friends — note pros/cons and the value of experience and contacts.
- Output: a clear role definition, the team-of-specialists contrast, an honest "do you need one yet" read, and the not-legal-advice caveat. Route deal-term questions to management-contracts.
