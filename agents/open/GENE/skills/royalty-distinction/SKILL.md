# Royalty Distinction: Composition vs Master

This is GENE's bedrock concept for music money. Every dollar in recorded music traces back to one of two separate copyrights that live in the same recording: the **musical composition** (the underlying song — melody and lyrics) and the **sound recording** (the master — one specific captured performance of that song). Different people can own each, different royalties flow from each, and confusing the two is the single most common cause of artists losing money. This skill teaches GENE to always identify which copyright is in play before talking about who gets paid. Canonical reference: https://aristake.com/songwriter-royalties/

## Instructions

1. Establish the two copyrights up front: the **composition** ("the song") is the written work — melody, harmony, lyrics — owned by the songwriter(s) and their **publisher**. The **sound recording** ("the master") is a particular recorded performance of that song, owned by the **recording artist** and/or their **label**. A cover version is the same composition with a different master.
2. Map the two royalty families. From the **composition**: songwriter/publisher royalties — chiefly **performance royalties** (when the song is publicly performed/broadcast/streamed) and **mechanical royalties** (when the song is reproduced — physical, download, or stream). From the **master**: artist/label royalties — sales, the master share of streaming, and **digital performance royalties** for non-interactive digital/satellite play (collected via SoundExchange in the US).
3. Stress that one person can wear both hats. A self-written, self-released artist owns both copyrights and collects on both sides — but the streams are still accounted, collected, and paid through different pipes, so they must be claimed separately.
4. Name the collectors so the user knows where money hides: composition performance royalties come via a **PRO** (ASCAP/BMI/SESAC/GMR) and the publisher; **mechanicals** in the US come via **The MLC** (for streaming/download) and via the label/Harry Fox for physical; master digital-performance royalties come via **SoundExchange**.
5. When the user describes a scenario, force the question: *is this about the song or the recording?* Then trace each applicable royalty to its collector and its rightful owner.
6. Flag the classic leaks: songwriters who never registered with a PRO, writers who never set up publishing administration, and artists who signed away masters without understanding the master/composition split.
7. State plainly that this is general music-business education, not legal or accounting advice — real splits, contracts, and ownership should be confirmed in writing (a **split sheet** at the session) and reviewed by a music attorney.

## Examples

- User: "Someone covered my song on Spotify — do I get paid?" -> Yes, as the **songwriter** you own the composition: you're owed mechanical royalties (via The MLC) and performance royalties (via your PRO) on the cover. The person who recorded the cover owns *their* master and collects the master/artist side; you do not get their master royalties.
- User: "I produced and released my own single. What am I collecting?" -> You own both copyrights. Composition side: register the song with your PRO (performance) and The MLC (mechanical). Master side: register with SoundExchange (digital performance) and collect master streaming via your distributor. Walk them through claiming each separately.
- User: "My label says they own my royalties." -> Clarify which: a typical deal assigns the **master** to the label, but the **composition/publishing** usually stays with you as the songwriter unless a separate publishing deal was signed. Separate the two before agreeing.
- User: "What's a split sheet?" -> A document signed at the session that records each contributor's percentage of the **composition**. It prevents later disputes and is what publishers/PROs rely on to pay writers correctly.

## Guidelines

- Always identify the copyright (composition vs master) *before* naming a royalty or a payer — never skip this step.
- Do remind self-releasing artists that owning both copyrights still means registering with multiple bodies; nothing pays automatically.
- Do use plain labels: "song = composition = publishing side" and "recording = master = artist/label side."
- Don't conflate mechanical (reproduction of the composition) with master sales (sale of the recording) — they are different rights even on the same track.
- Edge case: samples and interpolations split *both* copyrights — clearing a sample means licensing the master AND the composition from potentially different owners.
- Output: a clear two-column mental model (composition vs master), the royalties on each side, who owns them, and who collects them — ending with the not-legal-advice caveat when ownership or contracts are at stake.
