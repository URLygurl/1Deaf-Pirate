# Stage Lighting Theory

Stage lighting theory is the foundation under every lighting design: what light is for, the properties a designer can control, and the angles that shape how subjects read. Whether the venue is a theatre or a festival mainstage, the same principles govern visibility, depth, mood, and focus. This skill helps Claude explain and apply the fundamentals correctly.

## Instructions
1. Start from the functions of stage lighting — the jobs light must do:
   - Visibility / selective visibility — the audience can see what matters, when it matters.
   - Revelation of form — modeling with angle and shadow so subjects look three-dimensional, not flat.
   - Focus / composition — drawing the eye to the right place on stage.
   - Mood / atmosphere — emotional tone via color, intensity, and contrast.
   - Establishing time/place and reinforcing rhythm/movement of the performance.
2. Work with the controllable qualities of light: intensity (brightness), color (hue/saturation and color temperature), direction/angle, distribution (size, shape, edge quality, gobo texture), and movement (change over time). Every design decision adjusts one of these.
3. Apply angle theory. Direction defines the look:
   - Front light — visibility, but flattens form if used alone.
   - Side / cross light — sculpts and reveals form; strong for bodies and dance.
   - Back light — separates subject from background, adds depth and rim.
   - Top / down light — isolation and pools.
   - Steep vs shallow angles change shadow and drama.
4. Teach the McCandless method as the classic modeling approach: light each area from two front directions roughly 45 degrees off center and 45 degrees in elevation, using a warm tone from one side and a cool tone from the other to create dimensional, naturalistic faces. Add back/side light for separation.
5. Address color: additive color mixing with light (not subtractive like paint), gels/color temperature, warm vs cool, and how saturation reads on skin and sets. Note that heavy saturation can flatten faces — balance with a less-saturated key.
6. Cover instrument basics: floods/washes (broad), Fresnels (soft-edge, adjustable), profiles/ellipsoidals (hard-edge, shutters, gobos), PARs (punchy oval beams), followspots, and moving lights as electronically controllable versions of these.
7. Connect theory to practice: every concert look is an application of these qualities and angles, even when automated fixtures replace fixed instruments.

## Examples
- "Explain the McCandless method." → Two front lights per area at ~45 degrees horizontal and vertical, one warm and one cool, producing dimensional, evenly modeled faces; add back light for depth.
- "Why do my performers look flat?" → Likely front-light-only with no modeling; add side and back light, vary angle, and introduce warm/cool front balance to restore three-dimensional form.
- "What are the controllable qualities of light?" → Intensity, color, direction/angle, distribution (size/shape/edge/texture), and movement — name each and how it serves visibility, form, focus, and mood.

## Guidelines
- Do ground every recommendation in a function (visibility, form, focus, mood) and a controllable quality.
- Do explain additive color mixing for light and warn that heavy saturation flattens faces — keep a balanced key.
- Do use angle deliberately: front for visibility, side for form, back for separation, top for isolation.
- Don't rely on front light alone; it flattens subjects.
- Don't conflate color mixing of light with paint pigments — they behave oppositely.
- Edge cases: camera/broadcast needs adequate, even key and controlled color temperature; very steep top light can hollow out eyes — fill or re-angle as needed.
- Output clear, correct explanations tied to practical application, not jargon for its own sake.
