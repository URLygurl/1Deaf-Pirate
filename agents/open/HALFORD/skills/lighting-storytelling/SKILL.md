# Lighting Storytelling

Lighting storytelling is the craft of using light to shape what the audience feels — building an emotional arc across a set, reinforcing each song's energy, and creating memorable moments through contrast. Great concert lighting isn't just visible and pretty; it breathes with the music. This skill helps Claude translate a setlist and an artist's intent into a deliberate sequence of looks, palettes, and dynamics.

## Instructions
1. Understand the music and intent: genre, tempo, the emotional shape of each song, and any signature moments the artist wants (a hush, a drop, a singalong, a reveal).
2. Map the whole-set arc, not just individual songs. Plan where the show breathes low so peaks land — a relentlessly bright set has no climax. Sketch an energy curve across the setlist.
3. Choose color with intent. Use color psychology as a starting point: warm ambers/reds for intimacy, passion, anger; deep blues for melancholy, calm, night; greens/cyans for unease or otherworldliness; whites/strobe for raw impact; saturated single-color washes for bold statements. Pick a palette per song and let palettes shift to signal sections.
4. Use contrast and dynamics as the core tool. Tension/release, dark/bright, still/moving, wide/isolated. The chorus only hits because the verse held back. Reserve your biggest tools (full back light, blinders, fast movement, strobe) for genuine peaks.
5. Design signature moments: a single top-light pool on the vocalist for a ballad, a blackout-to-blinders slam on a drop, a slow color bleed during a bridge, an audience wash for the singalong. Give each its own distinct look.
6. Match movement and timing to the music — slow, smooth moves for ballads; sharp, fast, on-the-beat moves for high-energy sections. Hit the accents; don't drift.
7. Keep the artist as the subject. Lighting serves the performer and the song; effects that bury the band or fight the vocal's visibility undercut the story.

## Examples
- "Design the lighting feel for a set that opens moody, builds to a banger, then closes on a ballad." → Open in deep blue low-key with sparse back light, escalate saturation/intensity/movement and add blinders into the banger peak, then strip back to a warm single top-light pool on the vocalist for the closing ballad; map the energy curve so each phase contrasts the last.
- "What color palette should this heartbreak ballad have?" → Deep blues and cold whites for melancholy, low intensity, a tight top-light isolation on the singer, minimal movement; maybe one warm amber accent on the emotional resolve.
- "The drop isn't hitting hard enough." → Hold the bar before it darker and stiller, then slam full back light + blinders + strobe + fast movement on the downbeat; contrast, not just brightness, makes it land.

## Guidelines
- Do design the full-set arc with deliberate lows so peaks have impact; contrast is everything.
- Do choose color and movement with intent and tie palette shifts to song sections.
- Do reserve your biggest looks (blinders, strobe, full rim, fast moves) for true climaxes.
- Don't light every song the same or run at full energy throughout — it flattens the emotional story.
- Don't let effects bury the artist or kill the vocalist's visibility; the performer is the subject.
- Edge cases: cover/festival slots with no rehearsal need a robust "busk" plan of go-to looks per energy level; respect photosensitivity — warn on strobe and provide tamer alternatives when needed.
- Output an arc/energy curve plus a per-song look brief (palette, intensity, movement, signature moment), aligned to the setlist.
