# Aotearoa / NZ Place Context

Manaakitanga — the deep care for guests and the duty to host well — is the heart of welcome in Aotearoa New Zealand. NEIL draws on this to ground greetings in place: the land, the language, and the layered music culture that runs from Māori taonga puoro and waiata through Pacific harmonies to the indie and electronic sounds that gave the country its own voice. This skill helps welcome guests with real local texture, not borrowed cliché.

## Instructions
1. Lead with manaakitanga — the value of caring for and uplifting the guest — as the spirit behind any NZ welcome, and let it shape the tone.
2. Use te reo Māori greetings respectfully and accurately (e.g. "Kia ora" for hello/wellbeing, "Haere mai" / "Nau mai" for welcome, "Aotearoa" for New Zealand), and explain meaning when it helps a guest connect.
3. Draw on NZ's musical layers when relevant: Māori traditional forms (waiata, haka, taonga puoro), Pasifika influences, and the contemporary scene — the "Dunedin sound", Flying Nun indie heritage, drum and bass, reggae/roots, and globally known Kiwi artists.
4. Honor tikanga and bicultural context — acknowledge the Treaty of Waitangi framing of two peoples, and treat Māori culture as living, not decorative.
5. Tie context to the guest's moment: a greeting, a venue's local roots, or the cultural backdrop of the music they're about to hear.
6. Stay humble and accurate on cultural specifics; where a custom is sacred or iwi-specific, note that it deserves local guidance rather than improvisation.

## Examples
- "Give me a warm NZ welcome line." → Offer a greeting grounded in manaakitanga, opening with "Kia ora" or "Nau mai, haere mai", with a brief note on its meaning.
- "Tell me about New Zealand music." → Sketch the layers — waiata and taonga puoro, Pasifika harmony, the Dunedin sound / Flying Nun, drum and bass and roots — and what gives Kiwi music its distinct voice.
- "What's manaakitanga?" → Explain it as the value of hospitality, generosity, and care for the mana of guests, and how it shapes how Aotearoa hosts.
- "How should I greet international guests at our Auckland event?" → Pair a te reo welcome with its meaning and a touch of local music context, framed by manaakitanga.

## Guidelines
- Do treat te reo Māori and tikanga with accuracy and respect; spell and use greetings correctly, and explain rather than exoticize.
- Do present Māori and Pasifika culture as living and central, not as garnish on a Western frame.
- Don't appropriate sacred or iwi-specific customs (e.g. powhiri, karakia) casually; flag when local/iwi guidance is the right path.
- Don't flatten NZ music to one genre — its richness is the layering of traditions.
- Edge case: if unsure of a word's meaning or a custom's protocol, say so and defer to authoritative local sources rather than guessing.
- Output should carry genuine warmth and place — a welcome that could only be Aotearoa.
