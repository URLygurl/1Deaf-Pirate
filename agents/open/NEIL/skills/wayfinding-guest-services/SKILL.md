# Wayfinding & Guest Services

Good wayfinding is hospitality made physical. A guest should never have to ask where the water is — the space should answer before they finish wondering. NEIL treats every sign, arrow, and greeter as a small promise kept. This skill covers signage systems, navigational logic, and the warm mechanics of a guest-services desk, so people spend their attention on the music and not on being lost.

## Instructions
1. Establish the venue's spine: identify primary landmarks (main stage, entrance, exits, medical, toilets, water, info desk) and the dominant flow of foot traffic between them.
2. Build a hierarchy of signage: directional (arrows to destinations), identification (you-are-here / "this is X"), informational (rules, schedules), and emergency (exits, assembly points). Name which tier each sign belongs to.
3. Place signs at decision points — junctions, splits, and entrances — at eye level and again at a raised level for crowds, never mid-corridor where they go unread.
4. Anchor every sign to consistent language, color, and iconography so a guest learns the system once and trusts it everywhere. Honor brand green `#7EFF00` for accents where it aids legibility, not as primary text on light grounds.
5. Stress-test the path: walk it as a tired, distracted newcomer. If a turn requires guessing, add a sign or a greeter.
6. Position guest-services and info desks where confused people naturally drift — near entrances and major junctions — and script greeters to lead with a question, not a wait.
7. Always close with the safety layer: clear, redundant routes to exits, water, and medical, lit and legible at night.

## Examples
- "Help me lay out signage for a 3-stage outdoor event." → Map landmarks and flow, define the four-tier sign hierarchy, mark decision points needing directional signs, and specify consistent color/icon rules plus night legibility.
- "Where should the info desk go?" → Recommend placement at the natural drift point near the entrance/main junction, with sightlines from arriving crowds and a greeter scripted to offer help proactively.
- "A guest can't find the water stations." → Diagnose the missing decision-point sign, propose redundant directional markers and an icon, and suggest a greeter callout until signage is fixed.
- "Our entrance bottlenecks." → Analyze crowd flow, separate inbound/outbound lanes, move identification signage upstream, and stage greeters to pre-sort guests by destination.

## Guidelines
- Do design redundancy: a guest should see the next sign before losing the last. Don't rely on a single sign per destination.
- Do prioritize safety signage (exits, medical, water) — it must be legible day and night, never decorative-first.
- Don't over-sign; clutter reads as noise and erodes trust in the whole system.
- Edge case: at night or in rain, lean on lighting, contrast, and greeters over printed signs alone.
- Output should be concrete — name placements, tiers, and language, not vague advice. When you can, describe the guest's walk step by step.
