# Mixing Essentials

This skill gives EDDIE an opinionated, order-of-operations workflow for turning a raw multitrack into a balanced, translatable mix. It covers gain staging, static balance, panning, corrective and creative EQ, dynamics, time-based effects, and automation. Apply it whenever someone is working on a mix-in-progress or doesn't know where to start. Canonical reference: https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mixing-essentials

## Instructions

1. Establish the goal first. Ask for genre, a reference track, and what's bothering them. A mix is judged against intent, not in a vacuum.
2. Gain stage before anything else. Get every channel peaking roughly -18 to -12 dBFS so plugins and the bus have headroom. Never start with faders pinned near 0.
3. Build a static balance with faders only — no EQ, no compression yet. Start from the most important element (usually lead vocal or kick/snare), then bring everything else up around it. If the mix works in mono with just faders, you're 70% done.
4. Check in mono early and often. Mono reveals masking and phase problems that stereo hides.
5. Pan for width and to clear the center. Keep kick, bass, lead vocal, and snare centered; spread guitars, keys, overheads, backing vocals. Use the "balance the picture" approach — mirror placements left/right.
6. EQ subtractively first: high-pass non-bass tracks to remove rumble, then cut problem resonances (boxy 200-500 Hz, harsh 2-5 kHz, mud) before boosting. Carve space so two instruments aren't fighting the same band.
7. Compress to control dynamics and glue, not by reflex. Tame the loudest performances, even out vocals, add punch to drums. If you can't hear what it's doing, you probably don't need it. (Defer to compression-theory for parameter detail.)
8. Add depth with reverb and delay on sends, not inserts — a couple of shared reverbs (short room + longer hall) keep the mix cohesive. Closer/louder = drier; farther = wetter.
9. Use a bus/mix-bus chain sparingly for glue (gentle bus compression, subtle EQ). Don't fix individual problems on the master.
10. Automate to keep the mix alive: ride vocal levels phrase by phrase, lift hooks, pull back verses, automate FX sends for emphasis.
11. Reference constantly. A/B against commercial tracks at matched loudness, and check on multiple systems (monitors, headphones, phone/laptop speaker).
12. Leave headroom for mastering — aim for roughly -6 to -3 dBFS peaks on the mix bus and do NOT slam a limiter on the master if it's going to be mastered.

## Examples

- User: "My mix sounds muddy and everything's fighting." → Diagnose masking: have them high-pass everything that isn't bass/kick, then cut 200-500 Hz on the worst offenders (often guitars/pads/room), and check the low end in mono. Confirm only one or two elements own the sub.
- User: "Where do I even start with 40 tracks?" → Walk them through gain staging, grouping (drums, bass, gtrs, vox, FX) into buses, then building a fader-only static balance starting from the lead vocal before touching a single plugin.
- User: "Vocals keep getting buried in the chorus." → Suggest level automation to ride the vocal, a narrow cut in competing instruments around the vocal's presence range (2-4 kHz), light vocal compression for consistency, and checking the balance in mono.
- User: "How wide should I pan my guitars?" → Recommend hard or near-hard L/R for doubled rhythm guitars, keep the center clear for vocal/bass/kick/snare, and balance the stereo picture so energy isn't lopsided.

## Guidelines

- Do start with subtraction (faders, HPF, cuts) before addition (boosts, FX). Most amateur mixes are over-processed.
- Do work at moderate, consistent monitoring levels and take ear breaks; loud listening flatters everything.
- Don't reach for plugins to fix what a fader, a pan, or a better arrangement edit would solve.
- Don't master while mixing — leave headroom and keep the two stages separate.
- Edge case: if the recording is fundamentally flawed (bad source, noise, phase issues), say so honestly — mixing can't rescue a broken capture (see mics).
- Output: give concrete, ordered, frequency-and-dB-specific advice tied to what the user described, and always recommend a mono check and a reference track.
